How to prove a video is the original

“Original” can mean two things: the first, unmodified file, or footage of something that really happened. You can prove a lot about the first. The second needs more than any file can carry.

What you can prove

  • That a file is unchanged. A fingerprint (SHA-256 hash) of the file matches only that exact file. If your copy matches a fingerprint published earlier, it's the same file, byte for byte.
  • That it existed by a date. A timestamp anchored in a public block chain, like an OpenTimestamps proof, shows the fingerprint existed no later than a given block. Nobody, including you, can backdate it.
  • Its order relative to other versions. If your file was stamped before an edited or AI-altered version circulated, your version can't have been made from that one.

What you can't prove from the file alone

  • That it shows what really happened. A staged or generated video stamped today is still staged or generated.
  • Who recorded it, or where. File metadata (camera model, GPS) is easy to edit. Content Credentials, where present, add signed claims about capture and edits; see timestamps and Content Credentials.
  • When it was recorded, as opposed to when it existed by. Stamp as soon after recording as you can to close that gap.

Habits that make footage defensible

  1. Keep the original file untouched. Copy it off the device as it is. Edit copies, never the original. Messaging apps and social platforms re-encode what you send, so the copy they deliver isn't your original.
  2. Stamp it early. Stamp the original as soon as you have it, before you share or edit anything. Keep the .ots proof next to the file.
  3. Write down the circumstances. Who recorded it, on what device, where and when, and who has handled the file since. Stamp those notes too.
  4. Publish the fingerprint with the footage. Posting the check link, or just the SHA-256, alongside a video lets anyone holding the original confirm it later.
  5. When challenged, offer the original and the proof. Anyone can check them on this site or with the official client, without trusting you or this site.

When the question is “is this real?”

Timestamps and fingerprints answer “is this the same file, and how old is it at least?”. Whether events happened as shown is a job for corroboration: other footage of the same moment, witnesses, location and weather checks, and the person who recorded it. A timestamp helps by ruling out later fabrication of the file itself.
