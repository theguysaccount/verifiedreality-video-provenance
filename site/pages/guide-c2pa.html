Timestamps and Content Credentials

They answer different questions. Content Credentials say how a piece of media was made and changed, according to whoever signed them. A timestamp says a particular file existed by a particular time, according to Bitcoin.

Content Credentials (C2PA)

The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) publishes an open standard for attaching provenance information to media. In its own words, Content Credentials “function like a nutrition label for digital content”: cryptographically signed statements about the capturing device or software, edits, and who signed them, carried with the file. You can inspect them with the Content Credentials verify tool.

  • Strength: rich detail about origin and edit history, signed by a camera, app or organisation.
  • Limits: you trust the signer; the credentials travel in the file's metadata, which some apps and platforms remove when they re-save a file; and most footage in circulation doesn't have them yet.

Timestamps (OpenTimestamps)

  • Strength: anyone can create one, for any file, for free; nobody can backdate it; verifying it needs only Bitcoin, not trust in a company or a signer.
  • Limits: it says nothing about the content, the device or the author; it covers one exact file, so re-encoded copies don't match.

Together

A timestamp can cover a file that carries Content Credentials, fixing the whole thing, signatures included, in time. That answers a question the credentials alone can't: whether the signed file existed before a dispute or a manipulated version appeared. If a file has credentials, stamp it as it is, without re-saving it, so the credentials stay intact.
