      How it works

      1. Fingerprint. Your browser computes the file's SHA-256 hash: 64 characters that change completely if a single byte of the file changes.
      2. Anchor. The page mixes the fingerprint with a random number, hashes it again and sends only that to the public OpenTimestamps calendars. They combine it with everyone else's submissions into one hash that goes into a Bitcoin transaction, usually within a few hours.
      3. Check. The proof (.ots) links your file's fingerprint, step by step, to a Bitcoin block. Anyone can verify it on the check page or with the official client, and compare their copy of the file.

      See a sample proof for this 4-second sample clip, then drop the clip on the check page to compare it.

      What a timestamp proves, and what it doesn't

      • It proves that this exact file existed no later than the time of a Bitcoin block (block times are accurate to within an hour or two).
      • It doesn't prove that the video is true, who made it, where, or when it was recorded. A fake stamped today is a fake with a timestamp.
      • It covers one exact file. Re-encoded, trimmed or edited copies don't match. That's the point: no one can quietly change a stamped file.

      Its real use is order of events: footage stamped before a manipulated version of it appeared, or before a dispute began, can be shown to predate them. More on how it works and its limits
